  Note-taking (sometimes written as notetaking or note taking) is the practice of recording information from different sources and platforms. By taking notes, the writer records the essence of the information, freeing their mind from having to recall everything. Notes are commonly drawn from a transient source, such as an oral discussion at a meeting, or a lecture (notes of a meeting are usually called minutes), in which case the notes may be the only record of the event. Since the advent of writing and literacy, notes traditionally were almost always handwritten (often in notebooks), but the introduction of notetaking software and websites has made digital notetaking possible and widespread. Note-taking is a foundational skill in personal knowledge management.

Note-taking is the practice of recording information from various sources to capture essential content and free the mind from having to remember everything.

## History
- Ancient Greeks created *hypomnema* for personal records.
- Renaissance and early modern students produced elaborate notebooks for later reference.
- Pre‑digital era used notebooks, commonplace books, waste books, and marginalia.
- John Locke devised an indexing system that inspired many commonplace‑book templates, including Bell's Common‑Place Book (London, 1770).

## Cognitive psychology
- Note‑taking involves acquiring, filtering, organizing, comprehending, and storing information, resulting in a knowledge representation and memory storage.
- Studies show handwritten notes lead to better exam performance than typed notes, likely due to deeper processing through selective rephrasing rather than verbatim transcription.

## Reasons for note‑taking
- Enhances learning and memory by allowing selective re‑organization of ideas.
- Re‑worded notes improve understanding of lecture content.
- Supports vocabulary acquisition and autonomous learning.

## Systems
- Various formats help structure information for later retrieval.
- Speech rate averages 2–3 words/sec (120‑180 wpm); handwriting speed averages 0.2–0.3 words/sec (12‑18 wpm).
- Note‑taking methods can be **linear** or **non‑linear**, often combined.
- Focus on capturing the most important information first.

## Linear note‑taking
- Records information in the order received; often chronological outlines.
- **Outlining** uses hierarchical headings (Roman numerals, letters, Arabic numerals) to structure topics.
- **Sentence method** writes each idea as a short sentence on a separate line, without hierarchical organization.

## Non‑linear note‑taking
- Includes clustering, concept mapping, Cornell Notes, idea mapping, Ishikawa diagrams, knowledge maps, learning maps, mind mapping, model maps, and the pyramid principle.
- **Charting** creates tables to compare categories (e.g., similarities, differences).
- **Mapping** uses spatial diagrams; mind maps branch from a central concept.
- **Cornell Notes** (Walter Pauk, 1974) divides a page into notes, cues, and a summary strip.
- **SQ3R** (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) is a reading‑oriented note method; SQ4R adds a *Relate* step.
- **Guided notes** provide a framework with missing points for students to fill in during lectures, improving engagement and performance.
- **Card file** employs index cards (or digital equivalents) linked by headings or tags.

## Electronic note‑taking methods
- Laptops, tablets, and PDAs are widely used; word processors, graphics tablets, and dedicated software support digital notes.
- Online platforms enable sharing, collaboration, and email forwarding.
- Concerns exist over copyright, intellectual‑property, and AI‑generated notes (e.g., Turbo A.I., StudyCu, Stydra).
- Research (Mueller & Oppenheimer) indicates digital typing may reduce retention compared to paper, though other studies (Luo et al.) find benefits at certain learning stages.
- Multitasking on laptops can be distracting; benefits differ between typing on laptops vs. writing on tablets.

## Professional services
- Professional note‑takers assist individuals unable to take notes themselves, such as deaf or hearing‑impaired persons, in academic, corporate, and conference settings.
