  The Pomodoro Technique is a time management method developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. It uses a kitchen timer to break work into intervals, typically 25 minutes in length, separated by short breaks. Each interval is known as a pomodoro, from the Italian word for tomato, after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer that Cirillo used while he was a university student. Apps and websites providing timers and instructions have popularized the technique. Closely related to concepts such as timeboxing and iterative and incremental development used in software design, the method has been adopted in pair programming contexts.

The Pomodoro Technique is a time‑management method created by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s that breaks work into timed intervals called pomodori.

## Description
- The method: decide on a task, set a 25‑minute timer, work until it rings, then take a short 5–10 minute break. After four pomodori, take a longer 20–30 minute break before starting a new cycle.
- A pomodoro is an indivisible work interval; interruptions must be recorded and postponed or the pomodoro abandoned.
- Core stages: planning (prioritizing tasks on a “To Do Today” list), tracking, recording, processing, and visualizing completed pomodori.

## Tools
- Recommended low‑tech setup: mechanical kitchen timer, paper, and pencil. The act of winding the timer reinforces commitment; the ticking and ringing provide external cues for focus and breaks.
- Numerous software applications and web timers have been developed to implement the technique digitally.

## Research
- One study compared 25 students using Pomodoro with 35 students taking self‑regulated breaks. Self‑regulated breakers opted for longer sessions, reporting higher fatigue and lower concentration and motivation. No differences were found in mental effort or task completion between groups.
- A 2025 scoping review reported that “time‑structured Pomodoro interventions consistently improved focus, reduced mental fatigue, and enhanced sustained task performance, outperforming self‑paced breaks.”
