The main argument is that strategically designed quizzes—pre‑questions to activate prior knowledge and closed‑book quizzes to re‑expose students to material—directly enhance learning and reduce forgetting.

## Key Concepts
- **Pre‑questions (Recommendation 5a)**: short questions posed before new content to activate prior knowledge and highlight key concepts.
- **Re‑exposure quizzes (Recommendation 5b)**: closed‑book quizzes used after instruction to reinforce retention; active recall (fill‑in‑the‑blank, short‑answer) is more effective than rereading.
- **Testing Effect**: the robust finding that taking a test improves long‑term memory more than additional study.
- **Roadblocks & Solutions**: common teacher concerns (e.g., giving away answers, time to grade) and practical mitigations (class discussion of alternatives, use of technology platforms).

## Detailed Notes
### Recommendation 5a – Use pre‑questions to introduce a new topic
- **Purpose**: Identify unknown material, activate relevant prior knowledge, focus attention.
- **Evidence level**: Low (laboratory experiments on reading, mostly college students; not yet tested in regular classroom settings).
- **Evidence summary**: Studies show pre‑questions improve learning of both expository and narrative texts; little evidence for oral instruction. When students are required to read all material, pre‑questions do not harm learning of non‑questioned content.
- **Practical guide**:
  - Pose a few essential pre‑questions covering key concepts of the upcoming lesson.
  - Avoid questions that highlight extraneous information.
  - Anticipate objection that pre‑questions give answers; counter by discussing correct/incorrect alternatives in class.
  - Example pre‑questions:
    * Middle school social studies: “What were the ancient doctors NOT able to do?” (options: give shots, cure illnesses, measure heart beats, fix broken bones).
    * World War II: “Why were people imprisoned in concentration camps?”
    * Neuroscience: “Normally, the left hemisphere processes what kind of information?”
  - Implementation tip: print several pre‑questions on a sheet for students to answer immediately upon taking their seats; require brief explanations rather than one‑word answers.
  - To prevent neglect of non‑questioned material, explicitly require students to read the entire assigned text.

### Recommendation 5b – Use quizzes to re‑expose students to information
- **Purpose**: Provide delayed re‑exposure, promote active recall, improve retention.
- **Evidence level**: Strong (nine K‑12 experimental studies, >30 college studies, extensive laboratory work on the testing effect).
- **Evidence summary**: The testing effect is robust across material types (words, pictures, prose) and ages; intervening tests that differ in format from the final test still boost performance. Active recall via short‑answer or fill‑in‑the‑blank items yields greater learning than additional study.
- **Practical guide**:
  - Use closed‑book quizzes (fill‑in‑the‑blank, short‑answer) after instruction.
  - Provide correct‑answer feedback immediately.
  - Example study: college web‑based course where weekly quizzes outperformed rereading on both unit and final tests.
- **Roadblocks & Solutions**:
  - **Time to prepare/grade**: Leverage online quiz platforms (e.g., www.quia.com) that generate quizzes quickly and offer instant feedback, reducing grading burden.

| Term | Definition |
