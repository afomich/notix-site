We settle down in our new home, and I resolve to keep a diary.

My dear wife Carrie and I have just been a week in our new house, “The Laurels,” Brickfield Terrace, Holloway—a nice six‑roomed residence with a front breakfast‑parlour, a small front garden, and a ten‑step flight to the front door that we keep locked with a chain.  We use a side entrance for friends like Cummings, Gowing and others, sparing the servant the climb.  The back garden runs down to the railway; the landlord reduced the rent by £2 after we complained about the train noise.

I enjoy being at home after work in the City – “Home, Sweet Home” is my motto.  Friends drop in unannounced, but Carrie and I can also spend evenings together without them.  Everyday chores fill the time: fixing blinds, nailing a fan, repairing a carpet, while Carrie mends clothes and practices the “Sylvia Gavotte” on our new cottage piano made by W. Bilkson.  Our son Willie is doing well at the Bank in Oldham.

* * * *

**April 3** – Tradesmen visit; I promise Farmerson the ironmonger a turn if I need nails.  No key for our bedroom door; the parlour bell is broken, and the front door bell rings in the servant’s bedroom.  Gowing drops by but leaves quickly, complaining of paint smell.

**April 4** – More tradesmen.  I arrange with Horwin, a civil butcher, and order a shoulder of mutton.  Carrie orders a pound of fresh butter, a pound‑and‑half of salt, and a shilling’s worth of eggs from Borset the butterman.  Cummings shows me a meerschaum pipe he won, warning me not to get the hand moist.  He also leaves, disgusted by the paint smell, and trips over the scraper.

**April 5** – Two shoulders of mutton arrive; Carrie has ordered from another butcher without consulting me.  Gowing calls and also falls over the scraper.

**April 6** – Eggs are terrible; I send them back to Borset and tell him not to call again.  I lose my umbrella in the rain; Sarah says Mr Gowing must have taken it.  Later, drunk Borset confronts me in the hall; he swears he’ll never serve city clerks again.  I remark it’s possible for a city clerk to be a gentleman, which he seems pleased to hear.

**April 7** – Saturday; I’m delayed at work by ill principals, get home at seven.  Borset apologises repeatedly, offers a pound of fresh butter, and I order fresh eggs, demanding they be fresh.  I consider new stair‑carpets; Carrie suggests widening the paint.

**April 8** – Sunday after church the Curate joins us.  He can’t open the front door, so we use the side entrance; he trips on the scraper and tears his trousers, which Carrie can’t mend on a Sunday.  Later we discover a lovely spot for mustard‑and‑cress and radishes.

**April 9** – The butcher, whom we’d decided not to use, yells at me in public.  I shut the door and blame Carrie for the disgrace.  The butcher later kicks the door, injures his foot on the scraper, and threatens action.  I ask Farmerson the ironmonger to move the scraper and fix the bells.

**April 10** – Farmerson personally removes the scraper; he’s civil.  I complain about young clerks being late, warning three of them that Principal Perkupp might discharge them.

**April 11** – Mustard‑and‑cress and radishes haven’t sprouted yet.  I miss the 8:45 am bus after a quarrel with the grocer’s boy over a broken bell handle.  I’m half an hour late to the office, the only one, and Buckling the senior clerk saves me from reprimand.  Pitt the young clerk jokes about my lateness.

**April 12** – Still no sprouts.  Farmerson’s work on the scraper uncovers a gas pipe placed absurdly; I’m annoyed at the forthcoming expense.  In the evening Gowing and I smoke together; he offers a “green” cigar from America.  I joke about dry rot, which makes Carrie laugh heartily.

**April 13** – Carrie hires a woman to make chintz covers; I recognize her from my aunt’s former household.

**April 14** – I buy a cheap gardening book and sow half‑hardy annuals.  I joke with Carrie about having a “lodging‑house” because of our boarders; she retorts playfully.  Gowing comments the stairs look fine but the banisters need paint; Carrie agrees.

**April 15** – Sunday walk with Cummings, Gowing and friend Stillbrook.  At the gate of “The Cow and Hedge” the porter initially refuses us, but Stillbrook claims to be from Blackheath and we’re let in.  We wait an hour; Stillbrook apologises for keeping me waiting.

**April 16** – After work I garden; later I write to Cummings and Gowing about the gate incident but decide not to send it yet.

**April 17** – I draft a note warning Gowing and Cummings about Stillbrook, then tear it up, opting to speak quietly instead.  Cummings sends a sharp letter demanding an explanation; I reply with a lengthy apology, which I copy verbatim in the diary as perhaps my best‑crafted sentence.

**April 18** – I catch a cold, spend the day sneezing at the office, and send Sarah for a bottle of Kinahan.  Cummings drops by; later he offers me whisky from his cousin Merton’s trade, but Sarah interrupts with a cheap whisky‑and‑sherry offer.

**April 19** – Cummings brings Merton, a wine merchant, and Gowing calls.  Merton declares his motto “Few and True; Little and Good” for friends and wine.  He offers me a dozen “Lockanbar” whisky at 36 s., a good friend’s price.

**April 20** – Carrie reminds me that her school friend Annie Fullers (now Mrs James) and husband are visiting; I ask Merton for theatre passes for four.

**April 21** – Merton replies he can’t get opera passes but offers seats for four at the Tank Theatre’s “Brown Bushes” and includes a whisky bill.

**April 23** – The Jameses come for tea; we travel to the Tank Theatre via King’s Cross and the Angel.  Mr James insists on paying for everything, though I had paid for the tickets.

*The diary captures the minutiae of domestic life, the recurring nuisance of the scraper, the social interactions with neighbours and tradesmen, and the narrator’s habit of turning everyday annoyances into jokes and reflective commentary.*
