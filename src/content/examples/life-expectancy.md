Life expectancy has more than doubled worldwide since 1900, rising from an average of 32 years for newborns to 71 years by 2021. This increase is due to reduced child mortality and lower death rates at all ages, driven by advances in medicine, public health, nutrition, sanitation, and living standards. 

## Key Insights
- **Global rise**: By 2021 the global average exceeded 70 years, a dramatic increase from under half that figure two centuries earlier. All regions have seen steady growth.
- **Regional disparities**: In 2021 Nigeria’s life expectancy was 30 years lower than Japan’s, and within countries lower averages often accompany greater lifespan variation.
- **All‑age improvements**: Mortality declines are evident across every age group. For example, French 15‑year‑olds’ life expectancy rose from 58 years in 1816 to 83 years in 2021; 65‑year‑olds’ expectancy increased from 76 to 86 years over the same period.
- **Sex gap**: Women generally live longer than men, though the gap varies by country and era, widening during periods such as the World Wars and narrowing more recently. Causes include higher infant male mortality, male‑biased violence, and greater male susceptibility to chronic diseases linked to smoking and alcohol.
- **Broken limits**: Predicted maximum life expectancy limits have repeatedly been surpassed. By 2021 Hong Kong reached 88 years, the highest recorded.

## Data Sources & Methodology
- **Period life expectancy** summarizes age‑specific death rates for a given year, representing the average lifespan of a hypothetical cohort experiencing those rates throughout life.
- **Primary sources**: United Nations World Population Prospects (UN WPP), Human Mortality Database (HMD), Zijdeman et al. (2015), and Riley (2005). Pre‑1950 data combine HMD with Zijdeman; post‑1950 data rely on UN WPP. Regional estimates draw on additional databases (e.g., OECD.Stat, MOxLAD, Gapminder) and specific national studies.
